Create Your Own POS Using No-Code AppSheet (And What It Actually Takes to Pull It Off)

Last updated: August 2026 · 7 MIN READ

If you've spent any time looking at no-code tools, you've probably run into Google AppSheet - the platform that lets you turn a spreadsheet into a working app without writing code. It's a genuinely powerful idea, and "build my own POS" is one of the most common projects people try with it. Here's what that actually involves, what you get in return, and where it can trip you up.

What AppSheet actually is

AppSheet is a no-code app builder from Google. You connect it to a data source - usually a Google Sheet, sometimes Excel or a database - and it generates an app around that data: forms, buttons, views, and workflows, all configured through a visual editor instead of code. For a POS, the "data source" is where your products, sales, and inventory would live.

If you want to explore the platform directly, you can visit the official Google AppSheet website for its current features, documentation, and ways to get started.

The basic blueprint for a POS in AppSheet

Most DIY AppSheet POS builds follow roughly the same structure:

  1. Build the spreadsheet first. A Products sheet (name, price, category, stock, barcode), a Sales sheet (each transaction), a Sale Items sheet (line items per sale), and often a Cashiers sheet. This is the real backbone of the app - get the structure wrong and everything built on top of it inherits the problem.
  2. Import it into AppSheet. AppSheet reads the sheet structure and auto-generates a starter app with basic views.
  3. Build the "ring up a sale" flow. This is the part that takes real work: a form or view where you tap products, have AppSheet write rows into Sale Items, sum them into a Sales total, and ideally show a running cart total before the sale is saved.
  4. Add stock deduction. Not automatic - you write an AppSheet "bot" (their automation/workflow feature) that triggers when a sale is saved and subtracts quantities from the Products sheet.
  5. Add receipts. AppSheet doesn't have a native till-style receipt printer flow. Typically this means generating a PDF from a template and printing that, or emailing it - workable, but not the instant thermal-printer receipt you'd get from purpose-built POS software.
  6. Set up user roles. If more than one cashier will use it, you'll configure AppSheet's security filters so each person only sees what they should.

Where AppSheet genuinely shines for this

  • Total control over the data model. Since it's your spreadsheet, you can add any custom field or business logic you want - loyalty points, custom order statuses, whatever's specific to your operation.
  • It plugs into the rest of Google Workspace. Sales data lands directly in Sheets, so reporting, pivot tables, and integrations with other Google tools are straightforward.
  • Genuinely no-code. You're configuring, not programming - approachable if you're comfortable with spreadsheets and have some patience for trial and error.
  • Built-in automation ("bots"). Useful for things like low-stock email alerts or daily sales summaries once you've learned the automation editor.

Where it tends to fall short as a real checkout tool

  • It's not built primarily for speed at the counter. AppSheet's sync-based architecture can introduce noticeable delays, particularly when the app has a large dataset or complex expressions and workflows. For a slow trickle of sales that's often fine, but during busy periods even small delays can add up.
  • No native barcode-to-cart flow. You can add barcode scanning via the camera, but wiring it into a smooth "scan and it's instantly in the cart" experience takes real configuration work, and USB barcode scanners (which just type the code and hit enter) need extra handling to behave properly.
  • Costs can increase as your business grows. AppSheet offers different pricing options, but business use with multiple users can require paid plans, making the ongoing cost an important consideration. See current AppSheet pricing.
  • Google Sheets as a database has real limits. As your Sales sheet grows into the thousands of rows, both Sheets and the app built on it can start to slow down noticeably.
  • You're the support team. There's no vendor to call when a bot misfires or a formula breaks at checkout - it's on you to debug it, ideally not while a customer is waiting.
  • Offline use requires careful configuration. AppSheet supports offline use, but designing a reliable offline POS requires careful handling of synchronization, inventory changes, and conflicts when multiple devices reconnect. For a serious POS, these details need to be tested thoroughly before relying on offline operation.

Who this route actually makes sense for

Building a POS in AppSheet is a great fit if you enjoy building things and want an app tailored exactly to a specific, unusual workflow - a POS that also needs to do something ordinary POS software doesn't, like triggering a custom approval step, or feeding a very particular internal reporting format. It's a genuine app-building exercise, not a five-minute setup.

If what you actually need is a straightforward, reliable checkout system - ring up items, take payments, print receipts, and track stock - and you'd rather not spend the time building and maintaining it yourself, there's a much faster option: use a POS that's already built.
